## The challenge

IBM's annual Cost of a Data Breach study, produced in partnership with the Ponemon Institute, is one of the most widely cited pieces of research in the cybersecurity industry. For years, the format had been the same: a static PDF, country variants and a PR push that focused on point-in-time press coverage. The data was compelling. The experience of using it wasn't.

When asked to reimagine it, my team discovered the underlying research contained far more value than a PDF could surface. Breach costs vary dramatically by country, by industry, and by the specific risk factors present in any given organisation. Flattening all of that into a single static document meant most of the nuance was lost - to journalists, to prospective customers, and to IBM's own security teams.

When we showed them the value they were keeping locked away, IBM asked us to change that.

## The results

- **140% increase in unique visitors** to the Cost of a Data Breach landing page during the campaign, with the calculator as the top-performing asset
- **219% increase in validated leads** year-on-year by the second year of the programme
- **86% increase in revenue** generated from those leads
- **19% further increase in form completions** in 2020, continuing year-on-year growth in engagement
- Referenced internally as **best practice for visualising industry data** across IBM, and expanded to cover more languages and currencies

## My role: Strategy and programme lead

As VP, Creative Technology, I set the strategy and led the programme across four consecutive editions, from 2017 to 2020. Each cycle built on the last, pushing the research further from a document and closer to a platform. I worked alongside creative director Albert Lewis Jr., who led design, UX and data visualisation.

## The evolution

### 2017: from PDF to calculator

The first change was structural. We rebuilt the experience around a personalised calculator that let users tune the analysis to their own situation: country, industry, and a set of cost factors such as incident response posture, encryption use, CISO appointment and cloud migration. The output was a real-time breach cost estimate, shown on a gauge designed to make the number land.

That personalisation was also the key to its media value. Filterable data gave journalists a useful tool for localising the story, multiplying pitchable angles across sectors and geographies rather than forcing every outlet to run the same global average. The mobile flow and shareable social cards carried each user's own number into their feeds.

### 2018: a lead engine

With the calculator proven, the second year turned it into a commercial engine. We designed a clear value exchange: register once to get the full Ponemon report, the calculator and a personalised data security readiness assessment. The assessment scored an organisation's risk against its industry average, then routed it to recommended IBM solutions and a consultant.

We also added an international statistics module covering average cost, cost per record, time to identify and contain a breach and the top cost factors for each country, plus currency switching and share and embed tools for press and partners.

### 2019: the report becomes the experience

In year three, we stopped treating the website as a doorway to the PDF and made the report itself digital. The 2019 edition became a chaptered web experience: executive summary, key findings, complete findings and methodology. A highlights panel set the global averages beside the reader's own region.

The complete findings needed a consistent way to present dozens of figures, so we built a data visualisation system of reusable chart templates that let each finding be explored in place, not hunted for on page 40.

### 2020: a global, year-round platform

The final edition widened the lens. An interactive world map opened the experience across 17 countries and regions. The calculator showed how each factor raised or lowered cost against the global average, an animated industry view showed how costs had changed since 2015, and recommendations could be filtered by industry. Every path led on to IBM's security services, and the experience was available in more languages and currencies than ever.

We redesigned the downloadable report in the same visual system, so the PDF and the web experience finally told one story.
